01 Brief and context
A Mastercard banking operator integrated into one of Chile's largest retail chains was redesigning their physical branches. Within that project, they asked us to investigate a specific problem: the self-service kiosks installed in branches were generating unusually low NPS — but nobody knew why.
The assignment was diagnosis, not redesign. They needed to understand what was broken and how to prioritize it — with enough rigor to justify development investment.
02 Research strategy
I designed a three-method approach that would triangulate findings from different angles. The sequence was intentional: expert analysis provided the framework for interpreting verbatims, and both together shaped hypotheses for guerrilla testing.
1
Expert usability analysis
Custom framework for 6 kiosk dimensions: ergonomics, accessibility, content and flows, visual layout, control, and feedback and help.
2
NPS verbatim analysis
222 detractor comments, coded into two categories: pain (what's broken) and improvement (what they think they need).
3
Guerrilla testing in context
Testing on real kiosks, in real branches, with real customers — kiosk usability depends on physical context, not the lab.
03 Expert analysis
Overall compliance was 44.7% — below the 70% threshold for functional self-service interfaces. The two weakest dimensions:
Control and feedback — 25%
The critical gap
Users had no reliable way to understand the state of their actions, recover from errors, or find help.
Accessibility — 36.4%
Below minimum
Contrast ratios, touch target sizes, and legibility failed basic WCAG standards.
"The best-scoring dimensions — visual layout (57.7%) and ergonomics (54.2%) — were still below the 70% threshold. The kiosk looked acceptable but didn't work."
04 Verbatim analysis: 222 voices
The client had accumulated NPS data but never systematically analyzed the open-text feedback from detractors. I coded each comment separating those describing pure pain from those proposing improvements — two radically different types of information.

05 Guerrilla testing in context
I designed and executed guerrilla testing in two metropolitan branches during peak hours — contacting over 90 customers and recruiting 10 participants. Average session duration was 10 minutes, with the longest at 17 minutes (inexperienced user) and shortest at 5 (frequent user). That variance was itself a finding.

06 Diagnosis and recommendations
The primary finding: NPS problems weren't isolated incidents. They were different expressions of the same core issue — a kiosk that left users in uncertainty at every critical moment.
Each pain point traced back to one of three root causes:
1
Ambiguous labeling
Service names and document types didn't match user mental models. Solution: card sorting from verbatim.
2
Missing or generic feedback
Confirmation screens didn't explain what actually happened. Solution: rewrite all status screens with 3 questions: what happened, what does it mean, what do I do.
3
No recovery paths
Errors left users stranded. Solution: preview before printing, specific error messages, visible help button.

07 Learnings
Method triangulation produces better diagnosis
If I'd only done expert analysis, I'd have found interface problems but missed emotional context. Guerrilla testing alone could have fixed symptoms without addressing systemic root causes. The combination made the diagnosis credible and actionable.
User language is the fastest path to AI problems
Reading 222 verbatims was one of the most efficient research investments in the project. Open-text responses contained exactly the words users expected to see in the interface.
One user's problem is likely many users' problem
The client assumed low NPS meant isolated technical glitches. Research showed the opposite: every pain point was a systemic failure affecting a significant proportion of users — in predictable, fixable ways.
